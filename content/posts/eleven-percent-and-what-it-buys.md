<p class="lead-p">When we last wrote about Hawaii's ocean safety officers, they had been without a contract for a year and no successor was on the public record. That has changed. On <strong>July 1, 2026</strong>, an arbitration panel issued a final and binding award covering July 1, 2025 through June 30, 2029. The City and County of Honolulu put the cost items before its Council two weeks later. This article does one thing with those numbers: it asks what they are worth.</p>

<h2 class="section-head">What the Award Says</h2>

<p>Negotiations opened July 1, 2024. The Hawaii Labor Relations Board declared impasse on <strong>January 28, 2025</strong>. On September 8, 2025 the parties selected <strong>Frederic R. Horowitz, Esq.</strong> as neutral arbitrator and chair of the panel. Hearings ran in Honolulu from March 2 through March 5, 2026, and the decision came down on July 1, 2026. The agreement runs four years, from July 1, 2025 to June 30, 2029, which means its first year had already elapsed when it was written, and its second began the same day.</p>

<p>Alongside the wage schedule, the award carries a <strong>one-time lump sum of $2,000</strong>, effective retroactively to July 1, 2025, for every Unit 15 employee who is <strong>not eligible for step movements for the duration of the contract period</strong>, prorated for anyone less than full time. Step movement continues in all four years. The uniform maintenance allowance rises from <strong>$20.00 to $25.00 a month</strong>, and reimbursement for uniforms damaged in the line of duty rises from <strong>75 percent to 100 percent</strong> of actual replacement cost. Travel reimbursement moves to the federal allowances. On health coverage, the employer pays amounts generally equal to 60 percent of the premium for the HMSA 80-20 plan, capped at 90 percent of total premium, with either party able to reopen that article by written notice by January 31, 2027.</p>

<p>In Honolulu the award covers <strong>299 Water Safety Officers</strong>. The city put its four-year salary cost at <strong>$9,102,959</strong> including wage-related fringe benefits, plus <strong>$688,198</strong> in health fund costs across the first two years.</p>

  <div class="callout-label">What this article is comparing, and what it is not</div>
  <p>Everything below compares the <strong>salary schedule</strong> to <strong>prices</strong>. It is not a claim about any individual's paycheck. Step movement continues under this award, so an officer who moves a step in a given year receives more than the across-the-board figure. But on a ladder the 2022 arbitration panel described as twelve steps moving once every three years, most members do not move in most years, which is why we do not add steps to the across-the-board total as though everyone received both. The award itself acknowledges the members who will not move at all: that is who the $2,000 is for.</p>

<h2 class="section-head">A Number on a Schedule, and What It Buys</h2>

<p>A raise has two numbers. The first is the one in the contract. The second is what is left after prices take their share, and only the second one shows up in a grocery cart.</p>

<p>Start with the first. The four increases are 2.61, 2.49, 2.47 and 3.12 percent. Added together that is 10.69 percent, but that is not how a salary schedule works. Each increase applies to a base that already includes the ones before it, so they compound. Compounded, the four come to <strong>11.12 percent</strong>. Compounding works in the employee's favor here, and it adds four-tenths of a point.</p>

<p>Prices compound the same way, and over the same four years, which is the whole reason the comparison is fair. The measure is the Consumer Price Index for Urban Hawaii, published by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics every other month. It is the closest thing to an official answer to the question of what a dollar buys in Honolulu.</p>

<h2 class="section-head">What the Last Contract Actually Did</h2>

<p>Before asking what the new award will do, it is worth settling what the old one did, because that is finished and can be measured rather than projected.</p>

<p>The 2021 to 2025 agreement, which came out of the previous arbitration, raised the schedule 3 percent in 2022, 4 percent in 2023 and 4 percent in 2024, with a one-time 1 percent lump sum in the first year that did not touch the base. Compounded, the schedule rose <strong>11.40 percent</strong> across the four years.</p>

<p>Over those same four years, from July 2021 to July 2025, the Urban Hawaii price index rose from 298.820 to 348.334. That is <strong>16.57 percent</strong>.</p>

<h2 class="section-head">What the New Award Has to Beat</h2>

<p>Now the same arithmetic, forward.</p>

<p>An 11.12 percent increase spread over four years breaks even against inflation averaging <strong>2.67 percent a year</strong>. Below that, lifeguards gain ground. Above it, they lose ground. That number is the entire test.</p>

<p>Honolulu-area inflation over the twelve months ending May 2026 was <strong>5.1 percent</strong>.</p>

<p>The contract term is not hypothetical at its front end either. Its first year is already behind us. From July 2025, when the term began, through May 2026, the Urban Hawaii index rose <strong>5.48 percent</strong>. Year one of the award pays <strong>2.61 percent</strong>. In the ten months we can already measure, prices moved more than twice as far as the schedule did.</p>

<p>There is one more comparison worth making, and it is the plainest. The award this one replaces raised the schedule 11.40 percent. This one raises it 11.12 percent. <strong>The new award is smaller than the one it replaces</strong>. Over the life of that old contract, Honolulu prices rose at an average of 3.9 percent a year, and the raises did not keep up. Prices are currently running at 5.1 percent, and this award breaks even at 2.67 percent.</p>

<div class="data-callout">
  <div class="callout-label">Where that 5.1 percent came from</div>
  <p>Honolulu inflation did not sit still while this arbitration ran. Over the twelve months ending November 2025 it was 2.4 percent. Ending January 2026, still 2.4 percent. Ending March 2026, the month the hearings were held, 3.7 percent. Ending May 2026, 5.1 percent. It has more than doubled since late 2025.</p>

<h2 class="section-head">It Is Not Final Yet</h2>

<p>An arbitration award settles what the parties owe each other. It does not by itself move money. Under section 89-10(b) of the Hawaii Revised Statutes, quoted in the city's own transmittal to the Council, the legislative bodies "may approve or reject the cost items submitted to them, <strong>as a whole</strong>." If any one county rejects any cost item, <strong>all</strong> of them go back to the parties for further bargaining.</p>

<p><strong>Maui County moved first, and fast.</strong> Mayor Richard Bissen transmitted the cost items to the Council on July 9, 2026, writing that he was "respectfully requesting floor action as soon as possible due to time sensitivity." The two resolutions went straight to the full Council on <strong>July 24, 2026</strong>, skipping the committee stage that handled this unit's last contract. Both were <strong>adopted</strong>: Resolution 26-119 for employees included in the unit, and Resolution 26-120 for those excluded from it.</p>

<p>Maui's own summary of cost items lists the same four increases Honolulu filed, 2.61, 2.49, 2.47 and 3.12 percent, each with continued step movement. That is the first independent confirmation of the award's terms from a second county. Maui put its added cost at <strong>$2,846,372</strong> over the four years for included employees, and <strong>$96,724</strong> for those excluded.</p>

<p>Honolulu introduced Resolution 26-166 on July 15, 2026, and had not adopted it as of this writing. For comparison, the firefighters' cost items took from April 9 to May 13 to move from introduction to adoption in the same chamber.</p>

<p>So one county has appropriated its share and the rest have not yet. The numbers in this article are what the panel decided, what the employer filed, and what Maui has now approved. Any one council rejecting any single cost item still sends the whole package back to the parties.</p>

<div class="callout-dark">
  <span class="callout-label">The measure that matters</span>
  <p>Hawaii's lifeguards went a full year past the end of their contract before an arbitrator wrote them one, and the first year of the agreement they received had already elapsed by the time it was signed. What they were given raises the salary schedule 11.12 percent across four years, in a place where prices rose 5.1 percent in the last twelve months alone. The last contract raised the schedule 11.40 percent while prices rose 16.57 percent, and the people who guard the water finished it able to buy 4.4 percent less than when it started. Whether this one ends differently is not a question about generosity. It is a question about a single number, 2.67 percent a year, and whether Honolulu stays under it.</p>
