  <div class="callout-label">Correction, September 10, 2026: the wage comparison and starting pay</div>
  <p>An earlier version of this article said a starting firefighter's wage grew about 23.3 percent from 2015 to 2025 while Hawaii prices rose about 35.3 percent, called the 12-point difference purchasing power lost, and put the 2025 shortfall at about $6,442 a year. That comparison was wrong. It set two different pay ranges against each other. Its 2015 figure, $53,364, does not appear on the state's July 1, 2015 firefighter salary schedule; the closest entry is $53,352, the first step of range SR-17, while that schedule's recruit rate was $49,320. Its 2025 figure, $65,780, matches the recruit rate, range SR-15, which the July 1, 2025 schedule lists at $65,784. Measured within the same range, the schedules show wages up about 33 percent over those ten years (SR-15: $49,320 to $65,784; SR-17, first step: $53,352 to $71,136), so the 12-point gap largely disappears. A gap between two growth rates is also not the same thing as the share of buying power lost. We have replaced the section headed "The Slow Erosion" with a comparison that holds one pay range constant and uses the federal price index for urban Hawaii, and we have corrected the summary at the end of the article to match. The earlier version also paired a 2024 Firefighter I figure (about $69,000) with a 2025 recruit figure. We have updated both to the state schedule in effect since July 1, 2026: <strong>$67,752</strong> for a recruit and <strong>$73,272</strong> for a Firefighter I after probation. At those figures, qualifying for Maui's median home takes about four and a half times a Firefighter I's salary, not five.</p>
</div>

<p class="lead-p">Almost every conversation about firefighter pay eventually runs into the same wall: the cost of living in Hawaii. It gets mentioned, nodded at, and then set aside. This article does not set it aside. It puts the actual wage next to the actual cost of housing, groceries, and a mortgage in the state these firefighters serve, and lets the dollars speak.</p>

<p>None of what follows is unique to firefighters. A nurse, a teacher, and a road crew feel the same market. But firefighter pay is set by public contract, argued in public forums, and justified in public documents, so it is one of the few places where the cost-of-living question can be checked against real numbers.</p>

<h2 class="section-head">The Backdrop: The Most Expensive State in the Country</h2>

<p>The county's own compensation consultants put it in writing. In the MGT compensation study Maui County commissioned, the cost of living in Maui County is described as <strong>1.3 times higher</strong> than the U.S. average, with Hawaii ranked the <strong>4th most expensive state</strong> to live in. The study used that fact to argue for paying management competitively. The same fact applies to everyone who buys groceries in the same stores.</p>

<div class="divider">· ◆ ·</div>

<h2 class="section-head">What a Home Actually Costs</h2>

<p>Housing is where the gap becomes concrete. As of early 2026, the statewide median home value in Hawaii sits around <strong>$836,677</strong>, and on Maui the median single-family home has reached roughly <strong>$1,200,000</strong>. At a 30-year fixed mortgage rate near 6.38 percent, the income a lender wants to see before approving the loan is steep.</p>

</div>

<p>A Firefighter I on Maui earns <strong>$73,272</strong> a year after probation, and a recruit starts at <strong>$67,752</strong>, on the state schedule in effect since July 1, 2026. Set that against the numbers above. Qualifying for the statewide median home takes a little over three times a starting firefighter's salary. Qualifying for Maui's median home takes roughly <strong>four and a half times</strong> it. Even with heavy overtime stacked on top, the Maui median is, for most firefighters, out of reach at the current price.</p>

<h2 class="section-head">By the State's Own Yardstick</h2>

<p>Hawaii's housing agency publishes income guidelines that define who counts as low- or moderate-income for each county. Measured against those, a starting firefighter lands right at the edge of what the state itself classifies as moderate income.</p>

<div class="data-callout">
  <div class="callout-label">Maui County Income Guidelines · HHFDC 2025</div>
  <p>Maui area median family income (2025): <strong>$110,900</strong>.<br>A single person at 60% of median: <strong>$56,580</strong> (an affordable-housing eligibility threshold).<br>A single person at 70% of median: <strong>$66,010</strong>.<br>Recruit starting pay: <strong>$67,752</strong>.<br>Firefighter I starting pay (post-probation): <strong>$73,272</strong>.<br><br><em>Hawaii Housing Finance and Development Corporation, Maui County Income Schedule 2025; DHRD Bargaining Unit 11 salary schedule effective July 1, 2026.</em></p>
</div>

<p>A person who runs into burning buildings for a living starts a career earning just above the line the state draws for moderate-income assistance. That is not a rhetorical flourish. It is the state's own income schedule set next to the county's own wage schedule.</p>

<h2 class="section-head">The Slow Erosion: Wages vs. Prices Over Two Contracts</h2>

<p>The gap did not appear all at once. It widened year by year as prices outran wage adjustments. Take the last two firefighter contracts, which ran from July 1, 2019 through June 30, 2025, and hold one pay range constant: a Firefighter I at the first step of range SR-17. On the eve of the first of those contracts, that step paid <strong>$59,592</strong> a year. When the second one ended, it paid <strong>$69,060</strong>, an increase of <strong>15.9 percent</strong>. The state's schedules show the adjustments arriving as 2 percent in 2019, 2 percent in 2020, none in 2021, 3 percent in 2022, and 4 percent in each of 2023 and 2024. Over the same six years, from July 2019 to July 2025, consumer prices in urban Hawaii rose <strong>23.6 percent</strong>. That is a gap of about 7.7 percentage points, and it means the same paycheck bought about <strong>6.2 percent less</strong> at the end of those contracts than at the start, including through the pandemic years firefighters worked straight through.</p>

<div class="callout-dark">
  <span class="callout-label">The Shortfall, in Dollars</span>
  <p>To buy in July 2025 what $59,592 bought in July 2019, that same step would have needed to pay about <strong>$73,628</strong>. At the end of the second contract it paid <strong>$69,060</strong>. That is a gap of roughly <strong>$4,568 a year</strong>. The current contract has not closed it: its wage adjustments were 3.0 percent on July 1, 2025 and 3.0 percent on July 1, 2026 (the same step went from $71,136 to $73,272), while urban Hawaii prices rose <strong>5.6 percent</strong> from July 2025 to July 2026. And with no step advancement in the awarded contract for four years, there is no built-in mechanism to close it before 2029.<br><br><em>Wage figures from the DHRD Bargaining Unit 11 salary schedules effective July 1, 2018 through July 1, 2026, range SR-17, Step E. Prices from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers, Urban Hawaii, all items, not seasonally adjusted (series CUURS49FSA0): July 2019, 281.928; July 2025, 348.334; July 2026, 367.836.</em></p>
</div>

<div class="callout-dark">
  <span class="callout-label">Plain English</span>
  <p>Hawaii is the most expensive state in the country, and Maui is expensive even for Hawaii. A starting firefighter earns $67,752 to $73,272. The median Maui home costs about $1.2 million and takes roughly four and a half to five times that salary to qualify for. By the state's own income guidelines, a starting firefighter sits right at the moderate-income line. And across the last two contracts, from 2019 to 2025, the Firefighter I wage rose about 16 percent while urban Hawaii prices rose about 24 percent, so the same paycheck bought about 6 percent less at the end than at the start.</p>
